There's a particular kind of annoyance in seeing a charge on your statement, knowing you've seen the name before, and having no idea what it's for.

The usual advice is to "set a budget". That's not the problem. The problem is that the money has already left, quietly, on a schedule you agreed to once and then forgot about.

So here's the method I'd use if I had to do it by hand. It takes about twenty minutes, and you don't need an app.

---

**Start with three months of transactions, not a spreadsheet.**

Most of the leaks are recurring, so what you're looking for is a pattern rather than a category: the same merchant, a similar amount, on roughly the same day each month. Read the descriptions in order and highlight anything that repeats.

The ones that hide are annual. A yearly charge appears once and then vanishes for eleven months, so a one-month look-back misses them entirely. If you can get twelve months of history in front of you, do that instead.

**Look for three specific shapes.**

- The same merchant, same amount, forever. That's the classic forgotten subscription.
- The same merchant, a bigger amount than last time. Price creep is real and it's usually a small percentage you'd never notice.
- Two of you paying for the same thing. It's very common in a shared house or a relationship where you each signed up on your own card, and nobody merged the plan.

**Understand what kind of payment you're stopping.**

In New Zealand your statement mixes two things that get cancelled in different places. An automatic payment is set up by you through your bank, and you can usually see and stop it in your online banking. A direct debit is set up by the business, so you normally cancel it with them, and if they won't play ball you can ask your bank to block that debit.

Cancelling is the step people get stuck on, and it isn't always your fault.

---

**This is a known problem, not a personal failing.**

The Commerce Commission has said subscription traps are an area of active enforcement and focus, and that making cancellation deliberately difficult can breach the Fair Trading Act. Research quoted by RNZ earlier this year put the cost of dark patterns and unfair subscription models to New Zealanders at more than $60 million a year. There's also a member's bill aimed at making it as easy to cancel as it is to join, which tells you how one-sided the current setup is.

Which is a long way of saying: the friction is designed. A business that makes you phone up during office hours to cancel a $9.99 subscription is not confused about what it's doing.

---

**Doing this by hand is fine once. It's the doing-it-again that fails.**

The reason I ended up building something for this is that the manual version works exactly until the next free trial converts, or the next annual renewal lands, or the next price rise goes through unnoticed. Then you're back reading statements again.

[Parlo](https://getparlo.app/) reads your NZ bank accounts through Akahu, under the open banking framework, and looks for those three shapes specifically: the forgotten subscriptions, the creeping bills, and the things you and your partner are both paying for. You swipe through the findings and it counts what you've saved. It also keeps watching after you cancel, so a saving only counts once the charge has actually stopped.

It's not live yet, there's a waitlist, and it covers the major NZ banks. If you'd rather do it by hand, the method above is free and it works.
